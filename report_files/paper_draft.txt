TITLE: DeepFLAIR*: Evaluating U-Net Architectures for EPI-to-FLAIR* Synthesis

AUTHOR: Alexander Shen
Department of Neurology, Cedars-Sinai Medical Center, Los Angeles, CA

================================================================================
ABSTRACT
================================================================================

FLAIR* is a synthetic MRI contrast generated by combining two separate acquisitions, and can be generated from a single scan using deep learning. Current state-of-the-art image synthesis in medical imaging use a standard U-Net. We compare three transformer-based U-Net architectures (Attention U-Net, Swin-UNETR, and UNETR++ with Voxel-Focused Attention) to a baseline U-Net on a dataset of 89 subjects using a composite loss of MSE, SSIM, and gradient consistency. All models except UNETR++ achieve comparable test SSIM (~0.918-0.924), while UNETR++ produces visibly blurred outputs due to overly restricted attention windows. Our findings suggest that in medical image synthesis tasks, dataset size, memory constraints on batch size, and loss function design are dominant performance factors, consistent with conclusions drawn in segmentation literature. Future work should prioritize patch sampling strategies and loss weighting over architectural novelty.


================================================================================
1. INTRODUCTION
================================================================================

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a chronic inflammatory disease which presents lesions
in the brain. These lesions can be seen in Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI), and
are particularly visible in T2-FLAIR scans, where they appear as hyperintense
(brighter) against white brain matter [thompson2018diagnosis, filippi2011magnetic].
The Central Vein Sign (CVS) is a key biomarker which can be used to differentiate
MS lesions from common mimics [sati2016central, daboul2023effect]. CVS can be most
clearly seen in T2*-weighted magnitude 3D-EPI images, in which veins appear
hypointense. By combining the T2*-weighted and T2-FLAIR through image
multiplication, we can generate a FLAIR* image which demonstrates hyperintense
lesions with dark central veins [sati2012flair, sati2017rapid].

A single high quality MRI scan can take 25 minutes [ozutemiz2023seven]. Movement
during and between scans can lead to artifacts and misregistration, and long
periods in the scanner may not be accessible to all patients. Workflows which
require lengthy scans, combine multiple acquisitions, or involve offline
post-processing can restrict implementation in clinical settings. Emerging work
has offered deep learning solutions through image synthesis tasks such as
super-resolution [quah2023improving] and synthetic contrasts [genc2023deepswi,
baburyan2025deepflair].

Producing an accurate FLAIR* from the T2*-weighted magnitude images is
particularly difficult as it requires learning to suppress the hyperintense
cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) to hypointense while maintaining fine details of the
CVS. Regression-based encoder-decoder architectures, such as the U-Net, are well
suited to this task. These architectures provide the depth to learn complex
features while preserving details through skip connections. In this paper, we
compare the performance of four U-Net architectures on the FLAIR* synthesis task.

================================================================================
2. METHODS
================================================================================

We used the CAVS-pilot dataset (N=89) with a train/val/test split of 0.7, 0.1,
and 0.2 respectively. FLAIR* volumes were generated from T2* 3D-EPI and T2-FLAIR
acquisitions through linear image registration, interpolation, and image
multiplication [sati2012flair]. Each volume was individually intensity-normalized
to [0, 1], and padded to (320, 384, 320) voxels.

For training, (64, 64, 64) voxel patches were extracted randomly and augmented
with random flipping in the axial and sagittal directions. Gaussian blurring was
applied at three discrete levels (none, weak, medium) with equal probability.
Augmentation was not applied during validation or testing. Patches were passed
into each architecture with subject multiplication to balance epoch lengths to
32 steps (Table 1). During inference, sliding window patches of size (64, 64, 64)
were taken at stride 32, and blended with Gaussian overlap.

Loss was calculated through a composite objective function combining
mean-squared-error (MSE) and structural similarity (1-SSIM) [wang2004ssim]. A
gradient consistency term was also included, calculated as the L1 loss of finite
differences in each spatial dimension, averaged across axes. Gradient clipping
(norm <= 1.0) was applied. All loss weights were set equally to 1.0. Models were
optimized with Adam (lr=1e-4, beta1=0.9, beta2=0.999).

Models were trained until plateauing or reaching 300 epochs. The checkpoint
producing the lowest validation loss was used for evaluation on MSE and SSIM.
All experiments were conducted on an Ubuntu workstation with a 32-core 3.7 GHz
CPU, 256 GB memory, and four NVIDIA Quadro RTX 6000 GPUs.

In total, 4 architectures were tested: the baseline U-Net, Attention U-Net,
Swin-UNETR, and UNETR++ with Voxel-Focused Attention. The U-Net and Swin-UNETR
were additionally evaluated with a sigmoid final activation.

--- 2.1 Baseline U-Net ---

The baseline U-Net from the original DeepFLAIR* paper [baburyan2025deepflair] was
replicated in PyTorch. The encoder has four Double Convolutional layers with
BatchNorm and LeakyReLU, and average pooling steps in between. The bottleneck is
a full Double Convolution layer, and the decoder matches the encoder dimensions
with skip connections allowing features to propagate past the bottleneck. The
output goes to a 1x1x1 convolutional block followed by a ReLU activation. We
also evaluated a final Sigmoid activation. BatchNorm was used as larger batch
sizes are feasible due to the lower memory footprint. In the original paper, the
network was trained for 75 epochs using deterministic grid patching and
fine-tuned with Optuna at batch size 8.

--- 2.2 Attention U-Net ---

The Attention U-Net [oktay2018attention] introduces attention gates between the
skip connections of the encoder and decoder. These gates learn which encoder
features should be propagated or suppressed. For example, they can allow CVS
structure to pass through while attenuating hyperintense CSF. The gates are
computed as a linear combination of the skip connection x and gating signal g,
passed through ReLU, a 1x1x1 convolution, and Sigmoid to produce a spatial
attention mask alpha, which is multiplied elementwise with x. The Attention U-Net
is otherwise identical to the baseline U-Net.

--- 2.3 Swin-UNETR ---

Pure Vision Transformers (ViTs) compare each voxel to all others, giving
quadratic complexity in the number of voxels. They also lack the inherent local
spatial inductive bias of CNNs, requiring large datasets to learn spatial
relationships from scratch.

The Sliding-window U-Net Transformer (Swin-UNETR) [hatamizadeh2022swin,
liu2021swin] limits self-attention to local windows of (2,2,2) tokens, while a
shifted-window strategy allows each token to interact with new tokens across
layers, building a global receptive field. We used a feature size of 24. Due to
memory constraints, batch size was limited to 2. BatchNorm is unstable at small
batch sizes, so instance normalization was used throughout [ulyanov2017instance].

--- 2.4 UNETR++ with Voxel-Focused Attention ---

UNETR++ [shaker2024unetrpp] addresses ViT quadratic complexity through introducing Efficient Paired Attention (EPA) blocks. Each EPA block splits computation into parallel spatial and channel branches, sharing Q and K projections but with separate V:

  - Spatial branch (VFA): Standard UNETR++ relies on dimensionality reduction which risks information loss. By using Voxel Focused Attention [VFA] {{https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/15/20/11034 #NEW SOURCE}}, each voxel computes self-attention only within its
    local (3,3,3) neighborhood, reducing complexity from O(N^2) to O(27N), preserving representational power
  - Channel branch: Each channel is treated as a token, computing a C x C
    attention matrix to capture global feature relationships without spatial cost.

The outputs of both branches are then summed and projected. The encoder uses a
3-stage hierarchy with patch size 4, producing 16^3, 8^3, and 4^3 token grids,
with a standard U-NET CNN decoder and skip connections. The architecture has approximately 1.2M parameters, enabling training at batch size 32 alongside heavier models.

4. DISCUSSION
================================================================================

--- 4.1 Architecture Comparison ---

Across both validation loss and test metrics, the performance gap between
architectures was narrow. Swin-UNETR achieved the second-best
validation loss (0.0637) and matched U-Net (ReLU) on test SSIM (0.918), despite
having nearly 3x the parameters and requiring 8x smaller batch sizes due to
memory constraints. Attention U-Net, which adds soft attention gates to the skip
connections, did not improve over the plain U-Net on any metric, suggesting that
the attention mechanism did not provide meaningful benefit. UNETR++ produced the worst validation loss (~0.10) and visibly blurry
outputs, which we attribute to the (3x3x3) local VFA window being too small to
reason about local intensity structure, a limitation that matters more for
synthesis than segmentation.

These results echo the conclusions of Isensee et al. [isensee2021nnu], who showed
that a well-configured U-Net consistently outperforms more complex architectures
on medical image segmentation benchmarks. We find that this holds for synthesis
as well: the dominant bottlenecks in this task are dataset size, memory
constraints that limit effective batch size for transformer-based models, and complex learning tasks (such as CSF supression, CVS preservation), none
of which are addressed by architectural novelty alone. The most meaningful
performance gain came not from a change in architecture but from switching the
final activation from ReLU to Sigmoid, which constrained outputs to [0,1] and
yielded the best validation loss overall (0.0617). {{can you show a figure? or table?}}

--- 4.2 Loss Functions ---

The composite loss function for the baseline training used in DeepFLAIR* was
manually tuned [baburyan2025deepflair]. We trained models with equal weighting to
evaluate the efficacy of each loss component in image-to-image translation.

During training, validation loss was dominated by the SSIM term, while MSE and
gradient loss remained relatively low. This likely contributes to difficulty
learning CSF hypointensities in the ventricles and brainstem, which require the
model to invert the intensity of entire regions. Increasing MSE weight
empirically reduced MSE without worsening SSIM or gradient loss.

L1 loss was also tested to encourage learning of black background voxels. An
early Swin-UNETR experiment with BatchNorm and sigmoid failed to suppress
intensities with L1, while hard sigmoid succeeded. L1 also appeared to push CSF
intensities darker than the FLAIR* ground truth.

--- 4.3 Final Activation Functions ---

A ReLU activation is used at the output of the baseline U-Net. This constrains
output to positive values but does not cap outputs below 1.0. During validation,
predicted voxels occasionally exceeded 1.0. Empirically, this did not appear to
affect SSIM loss significantly.

During early training, some architectures produced mostly-black outputs (near-zero
activations). Recovery occurred within several epochs, likely due to gradient
signal from skip connections. Kaiming initialization may have stabilized early
training.

Preliminary Swin-UNETR experiments with instance norm and no final activation
successfully trained for 30 epochs with reasonable outputs, suggesting activation
functions may not be strictly necessary. With BatchNorm, sigmoid struggled to push
background voxels to zero even with L1 loss, while hard sigmoid succeeded.

--- 4.4 Learning Rate and Training Dynamics ---

The Optuna-tuned Adam parameters from [baburyan2025deepflair] (beta1=0.468,
beta2=0.94) appeared too aggressive for learning CSF hypointensities. Models
would briefly produce dark CSF patches, spiking the loss, then immediately revert. 
Standard Adam (beta1=0.9) was used for all reported experiments. No learning rate
scheduler was used; manually annealing the learning rate after plateau detection
would likely improve final performance.

--- 4.5 Limitations ---

The original DeepFLAIR* implementation uses deterministic grid patching at stride
32, yielding a fixed number of patches per subject. This was not feasible for
Swin-UNETR due to memory constraints and epoch length. Random patching was used
instead, which may reduce spatial coverage consistency but could improve
generalization.

Gaussian blending was used for sliding window inference. A Hann window would
theoretically reduce boundary artifacts but could not be verified within the scope
of this work. Optuna hyperparameter search was not applied to architectures
due to compute constraints.

--- 4.6 Future Work ---

Several architectures could warrant further investigation, including DADC layers (Dual
Attention and Depthwise Convolutions) for high-frequency texture preservation,
GAN-based training for perceptual sharpness, and diffusion models with appropriate
conditioning for stochastic synthesis. A learning rate scheduler (e.g.,
ReduceLROnPlateau) would likely yield further improvement for all architectures
tested. Optimization for each architecture with Optuna could also improve performance.

Beyond architectural changes, improvements to the training pipeline may yield stronger gains. Lesion-biased patch sampling, which weights patch centers toward hyperintense regions, could improve sharpness of MS lesions and CVS structure in synthesis outputs, which are underrepresented in random patches over the full brain volume. Anatomically-masked loss functions, computing MSE or SSIM only within brain tissue or lesion regions, could focus the optimization on clinically relevant areas rather than penalizing background voxels equally. Better tuning of the composite loss weights, particularly increasing the MSE term relative to SSIM, may also help the model learn regional intensity inversions such as CSF suppression.
